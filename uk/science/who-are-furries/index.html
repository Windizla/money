Who Are Furries? What Research Actually Shows

Section Furscience Updated Aug 2026 Reading time 14 min

Quick answer: furries are people who are fans of anthropomorphic animals — animals given human traits like speech, walking on two legs, personalities, and emotions. For most, it is a creative, social, identity-oriented hobby. Decades of survey research from the International Anthropomorphic Research Project (IARP, also known as "Furscience") paints a consistent picture: a predominantly young, creative, gender-diverse fan community with above-average rates of belonging and well-being — and a persistent popular reputation that has not caught up to the data.

A definition that actually works

Pop media often struggles to define furries. A useful working definition used by researchers is something like:

"A furry is a person who self-identifies as a fan of anthropomorphic animals — fictional or stylized animals with human characteristics, such as the ability to speak, walk on two legs, or express complex human emotion — and who, to some degree, sees this interest as an important part of their social or personal identity."

Three things make this better than the one-liners you see in headlines:

  • It's self-identified. Furry is not a diagnosis, a kink, or a formal membership. If you consider yourself part of the fandom, you are.
  • It doesn't require a fursuit. The vast majority of furries do not own a fursuit. Fursuiting is a popular but minority activity within the fandom.
  • It centers anthropomorphism, not sex, costumes, or any single behavior. The unifying thread is a love for animal characters with personality.

This matters because almost every popular stereotype fails one or more of these points.

How big is the fandom?

Estimating fandom size is hard because there is no membership list. Researchers at IARP have used multiple methods — convention attendance, online platform activity, and survey triangulation — to produce rough estimates.

There is no single membership count, but triangulating convention attendance, online community size, and survey response rates, IARP researchers describe the active global fandom as numbering in the hundreds of thousands to low millions of participants (Plante, Reysen, Roberts & Gerbasi, 2016; see also the IARP Summer 2020 Survey report at furscience.com, which sampled more than 1,500 respondents from this population). The fandom has grown visibly since the mid-2010s alongside the broader growth of online fandom culture.

What we can say with more confidence:

  • The fandom has grown substantially since the early 2010s, in line with the growth of fandom culture online more broadly.
  • Major conventions now draw thousands of attendees. Anthrocon (Pittsburgh) and Midwest FurFest (Chicago) are the two largest in North America; Eurofurence is the largest in Europe; Confurgence is the largest in Australia.
  • Online communities are vastly larger than in-person attendance. Most furries participate online only, or only attend local/regional meets.

Demographics: age, gender, sexuality

Because fandom attendance skews toward people who can afford to travel to conventions, surveys conducted at cons have to be adjusted for demographic bias. But even with that caveat, the picture is unusually clear compared with most subcultures.

Age

Across IARP convention samples the average adult furry is between 23 and 28 years old: online samples average around 23 while convention-going samples skew slightly older (the 2020 IARP Summer Survey reported a mean of 28.3 years, SD 8.65). More than three-quarters of participants in IARP studies are 25 or younger, and about 88% are under 30 (Plante et al., 2016; furscience.com/research-findings/appendix-1-previous-research/summer-2020/).

The fandom is predominantly young adults, with a sizable tail of older long-time fans (often referred to as "greymuzzles") who have been in the fandom since the 1990s and early 2000s.

Gender diversity

One of the most striking findings in Furscience research is the high proportion of transgender, non-binary, and gender-nonconforming furries relative to the general population.

Gender diversity is unusually high. The IARP 2020 Summer Survey (which allowed multiple selections) found that about 12.5% of respondents identified as trans and a further 12.5% as non-binary, with smaller shares identifying as genderqueer (4.2%), genderfluid (5.6%), or agender (2.9%). Earlier convention samples reported roughly 2% trans and 10% genderqueer/non-binary (Plante et al., 2016; Wikipedia summary "Furry fandom", demographics section, citing IARP). Either way, the shares are many times higher than in the general population, which researchers attribute to the fandom being an unusually safe and welcoming space for gender exploration rather than to the fandom "causing" trans identity.

Researchers have noted that this isn't because "furries make people trans"; it is better explained by the fandom being an unusually welcoming space for young people who are already exploring gender identity. See our separate article on LGBTQ+ people in the fandom.

Sexual orientation

Similarly, furries identify as LGBTQ+ at rates well above general-population averages.

Only around one-third of furries identify as exclusively heterosexual; roughly two-thirds identify as gay, lesbian, bisexual, pansexual, asexual, or otherwise non-exclusively-heterosexual (Plante et al., 2016; see also Plante et al., 2019, Somewhere over the rainbow). Bisexual and pansexual identities are particularly over-represented, consistent with the fandom's emphasis on identity exploration.

Importantly, the fandom also has a higher-than-average share of people identifying as bisexual or pansexual specifically, a pattern that aligns with the community's emphasis on openness and identity exploration.

Why people become furries

Research on fandom motivation converges on a few recurring themes. Plante, Reysen, Roberts, and Gerbasi — the core IARP team — summarize them roughly as:

  1. Belonging. The fandom provides a community where people feel accepted. For many furries, this is the single strongest draw.
  2. Creativity. Art, writing, fursuit making, music, worldbuilding — the fandom is overwhelmingly creative and participatory, not just consumptive.
  3. Identity exploration. The fursona is a meaningful way to explore who you are, including aspects of yourself you might not feel safe expressing elsewhere.
  4. Anthropomorphism itself. Many furries genuinely just like the aesthetic and narrative appeal of animal characters. This is sometimes underrated: for some people, the answer to "why furries?" really is "because cool wolves."

A common misconception is to assume a single master explanation (it's about sex; it's about escapism; it's about trauma; etc.). The research does not support any single-cause theory. Different furries are there for different combinations of reasons, and those reasons shift over time.

Fursonas and identity

A fursona is an anthropomorphic animal character used as a personal avatar or self-representation within the fandom. Most furries have one; many have multiple.

Fursonas are a central research topic because they are unusual in fandom: you can be a Star Wars fan without creating a Jedi version of yourself, but for most furries, having a personal character is close to the default. Research suggests:

  • Fursonas typically reflect idealized traits — the version of yourself you'd like to be — rather than being completely disconnected from who you are. They are not "delusional" alter egos.
  • Over time, many furries report that traits they first expressed through their fursona (confidence, warmth, boldness) bleed into their daily self — a process psychologists call identity diffusion, which is normal and healthy.
  • Species choice is not arbitrary, but it is not deeply diagnostic either. Someone whose fursona is a wolf is not necessarily "dominant" or "aggressive"; species choice is personal and aesthetic as much as symbolic. See our species guide for more.

Well-being, belonging, and stigma

Because of how the fandom is portrayed in popular media, a lot of early work assumed furries would score poorly on measures of psychological well-being. The actual findings are more nuanced.

Contrary to early stereotypes, IARP studies find furries score in the normal range on standard measures of self-esteem, life satisfaction, and psychological well-being compared with non-furry controls, and on some measures of global-life satisfaction they score slightly higher than student comparison groups. The consistent moderator is belonging: furries who feel connected to the community and have furry friends show better well-being, while perceived stigma against furries is associated with higher distress — a pattern familiar from research on other stigmatized groups where community connection buffers minority stress (Reysen, Plante, Roberts & Gerbasi; summarized in Plante et al., 2016, ch. 7).

The pattern most consistently found is: furries who are connected to the community — who have friends in the fandom, attend meets or cons, feel a sense of belonging — score better than you'd expect given the stigma they face. Furry stigma itself is associated with higher rates of distress, but fandom community buffers that effect. This is consistent with a much wider literature on social support and marginalized groups.

We cover this in more detail in Mental health and support in the community.

Common misconceptions

Almost every popular myth about furries has been addressed in some way by the research. We've written a dedicated page for myths about furries and what science says, but the short versions:

  • "It's all about sex." No. Like any large fandom, there is adult content and adult members, but surveys consistently show that sexual motivations rank well below belonging, creativity, and aesthetic appreciation as reasons people participate.
  • "Furries think they are animals." No. The near-universal understanding among furries is that a fursona is a character, avatar, or creative self-representation. Therianthropy and otherkin identities are distinct and overlap only partially with the fandom.
  • "It's a fetish." Reductive and inaccurate. Adult interest exists for some subset of the fandom, but treating it as the organizing principle is like treating anime fandom as a fetish because some people are attracted to anime characters.
  • "It's just a phase." For many people it is a long-term identity. Fandom tenure of 10+ years is common among adult furries.
